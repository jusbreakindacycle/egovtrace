# National Mass Transit Master Plan
## Master Handoff Document

**Status:** Separate national planning/operations branch from eGovTrace
**Relationship:** Interoperable with the Philippine State Control & Assurance system, but not part of its core ontology
**Purpose:** Establish a national, multimodal, flood-resilient, accessible and service-oriented public transport system for the Philippines

---

## 1. Executive Handoff

The **National Mass Transit Master Plan (NMTMP)** is a national physical-systems and public-mobility planning framework for the Philippines.

Its central premise is simple:

> **Public transport must be treated as one national mobility system, not as disconnected projects, modes, operators, agencies and local routes.**

The plan therefore designs mobility around the **journey and the corridor**, rather than around a single vehicle type or isolated infrastructure project.

The system must connect:

**land use → population → jobs/services → travel demand → corridors → modes → stations/stops → transfers → fares → operations → maintenance → accessibility → safety → resilience → outcomes**

The plan is intended to operate across:

- Metro Manila
- highly urbanized cities
- regional centers
- secondary cities
- provincial towns
- rural municipalities
- island and coastal communities
- inter-island transport corridors

It must also interface with:

- freight movement
- disaster response
- evacuation
- agricultural logistics
- tourism
- healthcare access
- education access
- employment access
- land-use planning
- flood and climate resilience

---

# 2. Separation From eGovTrace

This document is intentionally separate from **eGovTrace / Philippine State Control & Assurance**.

### eGovTrace asks:

> **Is the State doing what it is supposed to do, with lawful authority and public money, and can the action be proven through evidence?**

### NMTMP asks:

> **Does the country's physical mobility system actually move people safely, affordably, efficiently and resiliently?**

They are interoperable but have different jobs.

NMTMP produces the **planning, network, operational and outcome model**.

eGovTrace provides an **accountability and evidence layer** capable of examining whether public authority, procurement, spending and implementation correspond to what was authorized and intended.

The two systems should exchange references and evidence, but neither should absorb the other into a single undifferentiated product.

---

# 3. National Mobility Problem Definition

The Philippines does not have a single mobility problem. It has a coupled system of problems:

1. road congestion
2. fragmented public transport services
3. unreliable transfers
4. weak intermodal integration
5. uneven regional mobility
6. excessive dependence on private vehicles in some corridors
7. poor first-mile/last-mile connections
8. unsafe pedestrian environments
9. accessibility barriers for persons with disabilities, older persons and other vulnerable users
10. weak integration between transport and land use
11. flood and disaster vulnerability
12. fragmented institutional responsibilities
13. weak service accountability in some operating environments
14. poor integration between passenger and freight movement
15. weak regional and provincial public-transit planning
16. incomplete operational data
17. infrastructure-first planning that can fail to deliver corresponding mobility outcomes

The master plan therefore treats **mobility as a system**, not merely as road or rail construction.

# 4. Core Planning Principle

## One Network, One Journey

A person should experience the transport system as one connected network even when multiple operators or modes are involved.

A journey may be:

**walk → feeder → bus → rail → bus → ferry → walk**

The institutional structure may remain fragmented where legally necessary, but the user experience should not be.

Integration must therefore occur across:

- route planning
- schedules
- transfers
- fares
- passenger information
- accessibility
- safety
- ticketing/payment
- service standards
- incident response
- infrastructure design
- data

---

# 5. Planning Unit: The Corridor

The principal planning unit should be the **mobility corridor**, not the isolated project.

A corridor is a movement system connecting origins and destinations and may contain several modes and service layers.

Examples of corridor logic include:

**barangay → municipal center → provincial capital → regional center → metropolitan gateway**

or

**residential area → employment district → CBD → airport/port**

or

**farm cluster → consolidation point → market → port**

Each corridor should be analyzed for:

- demand
- trip purpose
- peak and off-peak patterns
- population density
- employment density
- schools
- hospitals
- public services
- commercial activity
- freight interaction
- flood exposure
- evacuation importance
- available right-of-way
- operating speed
- travel-time reliability
- transfer requirements
- accessibility
- safety
- lifecycle cost

---

# 6. National Public Transit Hierarchy

The national network should use a **mode hierarchy**, while avoiding the mistake of treating one mode as universally superior.

### Tier 1 — Heavy / High-Capacity Rail

For corridors with sufficiently high and sustained demand, constrained right-of-way, strategic regional importance or major metropolitan movement requirements.

Potential roles:

- metropolitan trunk movement
- intercity passenger movement
- regional rail
- airport/port connections
- strategic high-volume corridors

### Tier 2 — BRT / Busway / High-Capacity Bus

For corridors where rail is not justified, cannot be delivered within reasonable lifecycle constraints, or where dedicated road space can deliver high-capacity service more effectively.

### Tier 3 — Conventional Bus

For medium-demand urban, intercity and provincial corridors.

### Tier 4 — Jeepney / Minibus / Community Transit

These modes should not simply be treated as legacy vehicles to be eliminated.

They can function as:

- feeders
- neighborhood services
- lower-demand corridor services
- flexible services in dispersed settlements

The question is the **network role and service standard**, not merely the vehicle type.

### Tier 5 — Ferries / Water Transit

Because the Philippines is an archipelago, water transport must be treated as a core national mobility component rather than an auxiliary mode.

It should connect:

- islands
- coastal towns
- metropolitan waterfronts
- ports
- island municipalities
- tourism corridors
- evacuation networks

### Tier 6 — Feeder and Demand-Responsive Services

For lower-density areas, rural settlements and first-mile/last-mile gaps.

Potential forms include:

- local buses
- community shuttles
- smaller vehicles
- demand-responsive services
- accessible paratransit where justified

---

# 7. Mode Selection Rule

No project should begin with:

> “We need a rail line.”

or

> “We need another road.”

The correct starting question is:

> **What mobility problem exists on this corridor, how much demand exists, what level of service is required, what geography and hazards constrain the corridor, and what mode or combination of modes delivers the required outcome at acceptable lifecycle cost and risk?**

Mode selection must consider:

- passenger demand
- trip distance
- population density
- employment density
- peak load
- right-of-way
- construction constraints
- operating cost
- maintenance requirements
- resilience
- environmental constraints
- accessibility
- safety
- implementation time
- lifecycle cost
- integration with existing services
- transfer requirements
- future land-use growth

---

# 8. Integrated National Network Architecture

The country should be modeled as an interconnected mobility network with several layers.

### Layer A — Urban Trunk Network

High-capacity movement inside major urban areas.

### Layer B — Regional Network

Connections between regional centers, major cities and strategic economic areas.

### Layer C — Provincial Network

Connections between municipalities, provincial capitals and local growth centers.

### Layer D — Rural Access Network

First-mile and local public transport serving dispersed settlements and essential services.

### Layer E — Maritime Network

Inter-island and coastal passenger connections.

### Layer F — Feeder Network

Local services feeding rail, BRT, bus terminals, ferry terminals and other trunk services.

### Layer G — Special-Purpose Mobility

Where justified:

- airport connections
- university/education shuttles
- hospital access services
- tourism transport
- disaster-response transport
- accessible mobility services

These layers should connect rather than operate as independent systems.

---

# 9. Integrated Fare Policy

The national objective is not merely a national payment application.

The objective is **fare integration**.

A passenger transferring between services should not face a fragmented payment experience merely because the journey crosses operators or modes.

The policy framework should evaluate:

- distance/fare principles
- transfer rules
- fare ceilings where appropriate
- concessions
- student/senior/PWD policies
- low-income accessibility
- intermodal transfers
- provincial affordability
- operating subsidy requirements
- revenue reconciliation

Technology may support this, but **payment technology is not the transport policy itself**.

---

# 10. Service-Based Contracting

The planning framework should move toward procuring and managing **transport service outcomes**, rather than assuming that ownership of a vehicle fleet is the same as delivering public mobility.

Where legally and institutionally appropriate, contracts can specify:

- route coverage
- frequency
- operating hours
- reliability
- vehicle standards
- accessibility
- safety
- cleanliness
- passenger information
- incident management
- service continuity
- reporting
- data obligations
- performance incentives
- penalties/remedies

The state therefore buys a defined **mobility service**, not merely buses or infrastructure.

Any actual contracting model must remain subject to Philippine law, procurement rules, fiscal constraints and the proper authority of the responsible institutions.

---

# 11. Public Transport Governance Model

The national plan must avoid both extremes:

**Extreme A:** centralize every route and operational decision nationally.

**Extreme B:** allow every locality to plan transportation independently with no network integration.

The intended architecture is **distributed governance with national interoperability standards**.

### National Level

Responsible for national policy, strategic corridors, standards, major infrastructure coordination, interoperability, national data standards and nationwide outcomes.

### Regional Level

Responsible for regional network coordination and integration across provinces and cities.

### Provincial / Metropolitan Level

Responsible for corridor planning and service integration within their geographic responsibility.

### Local Level

Responsible for localized routes, feeder services, land-use interfaces and local implementation subject to the national/regional network framework.

The exact institutional assignment must be validated against current Philippine law and administrative mandates before implementation.

---

# 12. Transport + Land Use Integration

Transit cannot be planned separately from where people live, work, study, shop and obtain services.

The NMTMP therefore links:

**land use → density → trip generation → corridor demand → transit capacity → station location → development pattern**

Transit-oriented development (TOD) should be treated as a planning instrument, not as an automatic real-estate strategy.

Station and corridor planning should consider:

- walkability
- mixed use
- housing
- jobs
- public services
- accessibility
- public-space quality
- displacement risk
- drainage/flood exposure
- emergency access
- local economic activity

---

# 13. Flood and Climate Resilience

A national transit network for the Philippines must assume that disruption will occur.

The objective is therefore not simply:

> “build flood-proof stations.”

It is:

> **design a mobility network that can continue operating, degrade gracefully, recover quickly and provide emergency mobility when parts of the network fail.**

The plan must model:

- flood exposure
- landslide exposure
- storm surge
- coastal hazards
- earthquakes
- volcanic hazards where relevant
- bridge vulnerability
- slope instability
- power dependency
- communications dependency
- road access dependency
- terminal dependency

For each critical corridor, planners should define:

**normal operation → degraded operation → emergency operation → recovery → restoration**

---

# 14. Transit as an Evacuation Backbone

The public transport system should also serve disaster response.

Major transit corridors, terminals, stations, ports and transfer nodes should be evaluated for their role in:

- evacuation
- emergency distribution
- medical access
- emergency personnel movement
- relief logistics
- temporary shelter access
- restoration of essential mobility

The evacuation role must not be assumed merely because a route exists.

It must be tested against:

- capacity
- travel time
- failure points
- access roads
- vehicle availability
- fuel/power availability
- communications
- accessible evacuation requirements
- crowd management

---

# 15. Accessibility by Design

Accessibility is a system requirement, not a special add-on.

The journey must be evaluated door-to-door:

**origin → sidewalk → stop/station → boarding → vehicle → transfer → destination**

Accessibility considerations include:

- persons with disabilities
- older persons
- children
- pregnant passengers
- passengers carrying goods
- emergency conditions
- low-vision and hearing accessibility
- safe pedestrian access

A station that is accessible internally but impossible to reach safely from the surrounding community does not provide an accessible journey.

---

# 16. Passenger Safety

Safety must be measured across the entire trip.

The framework should cover:

- road safety
- rail safety
- maritime safety
- pedestrian safety
- station safety
- vehicle condition
- driver/operator performance
- emergency response
- crowd management
- lighting and visibility
- crossing conditions
- reporting and investigation

Safety incidents must be linked to operational and infrastructure learning rather than treated only as isolated events.

---

# 17. Freight Interface

Passenger and freight systems must be coordinated without assuming that they should share every corridor or facility.

The national mobility model should therefore maintain a distinct **freight graph** that can be linked to the passenger network where dependencies exist.

Important interfaces include:

- ports
- airports
- logistics hubs
- agricultural production zones
- public markets
- industrial areas
- urban delivery corridors
- inter-island shipping
- farm-to-market roads

This is especially important because congestion is partly generated by the interaction between passenger and freight movements.

---

# 18. Regional and Provincial Transit

The national plan must not become a Metro Manila plan renamed as a national plan.

Different settlement patterns require different mobility architectures.

### Metropolitan areas

Focus on:

- high-frequency trunk service
- rail/BRT integration
- bus network reform
- feeder integration
- station access
- congestion management
- TOD

### Regional centers

Focus on:

- intercity/regional connections
- city bus systems
- terminal integration
- regional rail where justified
- provincial links

### Provincial corridors

Focus on:

- reliable bus services
- town-to-town connections
- integrated terminals
- ferry interfaces
- agricultural and employment access

### Rural and island communities

Focus on:

- basic service coverage
- feeder services
- community transit
- ferry networks
- essential-service access
- demand-responsive options where appropriate

The preferred approach is a federated model where existing systems remain authoritative for their own domains while interoperable references and derived datasets provide national visibility.

Core data domains include:

- geography
- population
- employment
- land use
- trip demand
- routes
- schedules

- operators
- contracts
- fares
- passenger counts
- travel time
- reliability
- incidents
- accessibility
- hazards
- infrastructure condition
- maintenance
- energy/power dependencies
- outcomes

The national mobility layer should preserve source provenance and timestamps.

# 20. Mobility Graph

The mobility graph should represent relationships such as:

**Person/Population → Origin → Trip → Corridor → Mode → Route → Stop/Station → Transfer → Destination**

and:

**Route → Operator → Contract → Vehicle/Fleet → Service Performance**

and:

**Infrastructure → Hazard → Dependency → Failure → Disruption → Recovery**

and:

**Transit Node → Land Use → Development → Demand Change**

The graph is an analytical representation, not a legal declaration of truth.

Every material analytical relationship must preserve source and time context.

- service schedules

# 22. Sensor and Operational Loop

Where technically and economically justified, critical corridors can use operational data from:

- traffic sensors
- passenger counters
- automatic vehicle location
- fare systems
- station systems
- flood sensors
- weather/hazard systems
- infrastructure monitoring
- incident reports

The loop becomes:

**OBSERVE → DETECT → ANALYZE → DECIDE → ACT → MEASURE → LEARN**

Automated detection may trigger alerts.

Human or legally authorized institutional actors retain responsibility for consequential decisions unless a clearly authorized automated control exists.

> **Did the intervention materially improve people's ability to move and access essential opportunities?**

# 27. Investment Decision Gate

Before a major mass-transit investment advances, the planning process should answer:

1. What mobility problem is being solved?
2. Who experiences the problem?
3. What is the demand now?
4. What will demand plausibly become?
5. What alternatives were considered?
6. Why was this mode selected?
7. What happens if the project is not built?
8. What happens if the project is delayed?
9. How does the project integrate with existing modes?
10. How does it integrate with land use?
11. How resilient is it to hazards?
12. What are its operating and maintenance requirements?
13. How does it affect accessibility?
14. What are the expected measurable outcomes?
15. How will those outcomes actually be verified?

A project should not be justified solely by construction value or political visibility.

---

# 28. Operations Before Ribbon-Cutting

A transport project is not complete when physical construction ends.

Readiness should include:

**infrastructure → equipment → staffing → operating procedures → safety certification → maintenance → passenger information → fare integration → emergency response → service launch → measured performance**

The system must therefore distinguish:

- construction complete
- technically complete
- operationally ready
- service commenced
- stabilized operations
- outcome achieved

---

# 29. Maintenance as a National Mobility Function

The national system must treat maintenance as part of the original investment lifecycle.

Every major mobility asset should have:

- asset identity
- responsible institution
- operating responsibility
- maintenance responsibility
- inspection regime
- condition data
- lifecycle plan
- funding requirements
- criticality classification
- dependency profile

Deferred maintenance should be visible as a system risk rather than hidden as a future problem.

---

# 30. Criticality and Dependency

Not every mobility asset is equally important.

The model should therefore represent **criticality**.

For example:

**Major interchange → regional bus network → hospital access**

A failure at the interchange may produce consequences beyond the physical facility itself.

Dependency modeling should identify:

- upstream dependencies
- downstream dependencies
- power dependencies
- communications dependencies
- road dependencies
- bridge dependencies
- flood-control dependencies
- terminal dependencies
- alternative-route availability

Criticality should support prioritization, resilience planning and emergency response.

It should not automatically become a legal or funding decision.

---

# 31. Decision and Automation Rule

Automation may:

- detect congestion
- identify service gaps
- detect disruption
- forecast demand
- recommend route changes
- simulate alternatives
- rank scenarios

Automation should not silently:

- cancel contracts
- blacklist operators
- suspend funding
- impose legal sanctions
- declare fraud
- declare corruption

Consequential action remains subject to the proper authority, evidence and review process.

---

# 32. Institutional Interoperability

The final institutional map must include all organizations whose mandates affect national mobility, including relevant national departments, attached agencies, government corporations, regional institutions, local governments, transport operators and infrastructure authorities.

The precise current roster, mandates and legal powers must be verified against authoritative Philippine sources before this handoff is used as an implementation mandate.

The architecture itself is institution-agnostic where necessary so that administrative reorganizations do not invalidate the network model.

---

# 33. Relationship to Flood, Water and Spatial Resilience Program

The NMTMP is a first-class pillar of the broader physical resilience architecture.

It connects to:

**water systems + drainage + flood hazard + roads + bridges + land use + transit + evacuation + logistics + public services**

A flooded road can become a transit failure.

A failed bridge can isolate a municipality.

A failed terminal can break a regional network.

A flood-control intervention can change the reliability of a transport corridor.

Therefore, transport cannot be planned independently from the physical systems on which it depends.

The long-term national network should resemble a hierarchy rather than a collection of unrelated projects:

**National gateways**

↓

**Metropolitan and regional trunk corridors**

↓

**Provincial corridors**

↓

**Local trunk services**

↓

**Feeder/community services**

with a parallel maritime layer:

**ports → ferry corridors → island terminals → local feeders**

and an emergency layer:

**evacuation routes → emergency transit → relief logistics → recovery transport**

# 35. Success Condition

The NMTMP succeeds only when public transportation becomes a dependable system rather than a collection of projects.

A successful national system should allow a person to:

- reach work reliably
- reach school reliably
- reach healthcare reliably
- move between cities and provinces
- travel between islands
- transfer between modes without unnecessary friction
- travel at an affordable cost
- travel safely
- travel with dignity and accessibility
- continue moving during moderate disruption
- evacuate during disasters

At national scale, the system should also improve:

- productivity
- regional economic integration
- access to opportunity
- resilience
- road-space efficiency
- public-service access
- logistics performance
- social and geographic mobility

# 36. Non-Negotiable Design Principles

1. **One network, one journey.**
2. **Corridors before isolated projects.**
3. **Mode-neutral planning.**
4. **Rail is a tool, not a religion.**
5. **BRT/busways are strategic high-capacity modes, not second-class substitutes.**
6. **Buses and local transit remain essential.**
7. **Ferries are core infrastructure for an archipelago.**
8. **Feeder services are part of the main network.**
9. **Fares must be integrated at the policy level.**
10. **Service quality matters as much as infrastructure.**
11. **Operations and maintenance begin at planning, not after construction.**
12. **Flood and hazard resilience must be designed into the whole journey.**
13. **Accessibility is door-to-door.**
14. **Passenger and freight systems must be coordinated.**
15. **Land use and transit planning must interact.**
16. **Regional Philippines is not a smaller version of Metro Manila.**
17. **Data must preserve provenance and time.**
18. **Observed reality must be distinguished from simulation and prediction.**
19. **Automation may recommend or detect; authorized institutions decide consequential action.**
20. **Projects are means; mobility outcomes are the end.**

# 37. Adversarial Guardrails

The master plan must be protected against recurring failure modes.

### Failure Mode 1: Rail-first thinking

**Correction:** begin with corridor demand and service requirement.

### Failure Mode 2: Infrastructure = mobility

**Correction:** measure actual passenger outcomes.

### Failure Mode 3: Metro Manila = Philippines

**Correction:** maintain differentiated metropolitan, regional, provincial, rural and island architectures.

### Failure Mode 4: One app = integration

**Correction:** integrate planning, service, fares, transfers and standards; technology is only one layer.

### Failure Mode 5: Build then operate

**Correction:** require operational-readiness planning before construction completion.

### Failure Mode 6: Ignore maintenance

**Correction:** include lifecycle funding and asset management from day one.

### Failure Mode 7: Ignore disasters

**Correction:** design degraded and emergency operating states.

### Failure Mode 8: Passenger-only thinking

**Correction:** model freight interfaces and logistics dependencies.

### Failure Mode 9: Autonomous punishment

**Correction:** signal → evidence → authorized review → decision → action.

### Failure Mode 10: Treat composite scores as truth

**Correction:** scores are analytical aids, not unquestionable facts or legal findings.

# 40. Final Handoff Statement

The National Mass Transit Master Plan should be treated as a **national physical mobility system**, not a list of railways, buses, roads or terminals.

Its core unit is the **journey**.

Its planning unit is the **corridor**.

Its network contains **multiple modes**.

Its governance is **distributed but interoperable**.

Its service is measured by **actual passenger and public outcomes**.

Its resilience is measured by **continuity, degradation and recovery**.

Its infrastructure is managed across the **full lifecycle**.

Its data architecture is **federated and evidence-aware**.

Its automation supports people and institutions rather than replacing lawful authority.

And its relationship with eGovTrace is deliberately clear:

> **NMTMP designs and evaluates the mobility system. eGovTrace helps determine whether the State lawfully and accountably planned, funded, procured, implemented and documented the interventions that were supposed to produce those mobility outcomes.**

This separation should remain intact as both programs develop.
