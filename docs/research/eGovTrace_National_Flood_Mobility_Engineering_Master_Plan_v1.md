# eGovTrace National Flood, Mobility, Resilience and Infrastructure Engineering Master Plan v1

**Status:** Research-to-engineering handoff.  
**Date baseline:** 2026-09-08.  
**Companion document:** eGovTrace_Whole_of_Philippine_Government_Responsibility_and_Resolution_Matrix_v1.md  
**Planning horizon:** aligned conceptually with the RA 12145 long-term framework ending in 2050.

## 1. Executive architecture

The Philippines does not need a rival “super master plan” outside the legal planning system. The engineering program defined here is intended to become substantive input to:

> **agency/sector master plans → DEPDev/InfraCom CIDMP → budget/procurement/project execution → operations/maintenance → outcome measurement → continuous revision**

The engineering objective is not “eliminate all floods” or “make every car move faster.” Those are physically and institutionally wrong goals for an archipelagic, hazard-exposed country.

The national objectives are:

1. **reduce avoidable flood exposure, depth, duration and critical-service failure;**
2. **protect and restore the natural systems that store, slow, convey and discharge water;**
3. **make high-quality public transport, walking and cycling the default mobility backbone in dense corridors;**
4. **connect islands and regions through coherent road, rail, ferry, port and aviation networks;**
5. **prevent housing, land-use and infrastructure decisions from creating new hazard and transport poverty;**
6. **keep hospitals, fire/rescue, evacuation, power, water, telecom and logistics accessible during hazards;**
7. **treat infrastructure as a lifecycle asset, not a ribbon-cutting event;**
8. **make planning, budget, procurement, construction, payment, maintenance and outcomes traceable without converting analytics into accusations.**

## 2. Engineering doctrines

### 2.1 Water is not an enemy to be defeated

National flood engineering must choose among four strategies based on risk, density, economics, ecology and residual hazard:

- **Protect** — dikes, levees, floodwalls, pumping, surge barriers and structural defenses where justified.
- **Accommodate** — floodable parks, retention spaces, elevated/robust structures, safe overflow paths and flood-compatible uses.
- **Restore** — wetlands, mangroves, river corridors, forests, natural floodplains, dunes and watershed function.
- **Retreat** — planned relocation or land-use change where repeated protection is physically, socially or economically indefensible.

The strategy can be hybrid. “Nature-based” does not mean “no engineering,” and concrete does not automatically mean resilience.

### 2.2 Public transport is traffic policy

For dense urban corridors, the engineering unit is **people moved and access provided**, not cars processed.

Default hierarchy:

1. walking;
2. universal accessibility;
3. safe cycling and micromobility where suitable;
4. high-capacity public transport;
5. other public transport and feeder services;
6. shared/service/freight access;
7. private-car capacity where justified after higher-capacity alternatives are addressed.

### 2.3 Plan by physical system, not only jurisdiction

Use nested planning units:

- watershed / river basin;
- floodplain / coastal cell;
- metropolitan functional urban area;
- transport corridor;
- island / island group;
- logistics catchment;
- critical-service accessibility catchment;
- administrative region/LGU for governance and budgeting.

A river does not stop at a city boundary. A commute does not stop at a city boundary. A power outage does not respect a project boundary.

### 2.4 Lifecycle before expansion

For every asset:

> **Need → Plan → Evaluate → Budget → Procure → Construct → Inspect → Accept → Operate → Maintain → Measure → Rehabilitate/Replace**

A system that builds faster than it maintains can become less resilient despite higher capital spending.

## 3. National system architecture

The engineering master plan is organized into five interlocking national systems.

### System A — National Infrastructure and Spatial Graph

Lead architecture: DEPDev + DICT + NAMRIA + PSA, with authoritative sector sources retained.

Purpose:

- one spatial reference for projects/assets without creating one ownership database;
- link population, economy, hazards, infrastructure, services and government projects;
- represent upstream/downstream and network dependencies;
- expose overlapping scopes and missing connections;
- support planning scenarios and outcome evaluation.

### System B — National Flood and Water Resilience System

Core actors: DPWH + DENR + DOST/PAGASA + NWRB + NIA + DHSUD + DILG/LGUs + OCD/NDRRMC + water utilities, with MMDA/LLDA and regional bodies where applicable.

Purpose:

- watershed-to-coast risk reduction;
- urban drainage and pumping;
- river/floodplain management;
- nature-based storage and coastal protection;
- reservoir/irrigation coordination;
- flood-safe settlement and critical services;
- event learning and maintenance.

### System C — National Multimodal Public Transport and Logistics System

Core actors: DOTr + DPWH + LGUs + LTFRB/LTO/OTC + rail + ports + MARINA/PCG + aviation/airports + metropolitan bodies.

Purpose:

- high-capacity people movement;
- seamless transfers;
- integrated ferries/ports for an archipelago;
- regional rail and buses;
- freight corridors/logistics hubs;
- safe walking/cycling;
- road network used according to function, not universal car priority.

### System D — National Settlement and Accessibility System

Core actors: DHSUD + NHA/SHFC/NHMFC + LGUs + DOTr + DPWH + DepEd + DOH + utilities + DSWD.

Purpose:

- prevent new housing in intolerable hazard zones;
- make housing and transport affordability one problem;
- link densification to transit and utility capacity;
- make relocation preserve jobs, schools, health, social networks and mobility.

### System E — National Infrastructure Lifecycle and Accountability System

Core actors: DBM + GPPB/PhilGEPS + DEPDev + implementing agencies; independent oversight by COA/Ombudsman/courts remains separate.

Purpose:

- connect plans to appropriations;
- connect appropriations to procurement/contracts;
- connect contracts to physical assets;
- connect milestones/inspections/payments to verified delivery;
- connect completed assets to maintenance and outcomes;
- surface discrepancies without declaring guilt.

## 4. Flood engineering: watershed to coast

### 4.1 National basin inventory

Every major basin requires a versioned model including:

- rainfall and climate scenarios;
- elevation/topography;
- soil/infiltration;
- forests and land cover;
- tributaries and river geometry;
- dams/reservoirs;
- irrigation canals;
- urban drainage;
- bridges/culverts and constrictions;
- floodplains and wetlands;
- settlements and critical facilities;
- groundwater/subsidence where relevant;
- estuary/tide;
- storm surge and coastal erosion.

Planning output: a basin risk map that shows **source → pathway → receptor → consequence**.

### 4.2 Upstream retention before downstream acceleration

Every proposed channel widening, dike, revetment, drainage outfall or pump must model downstream consequences.

Design question:

> Does this project reduce local water level by transferring a larger or faster flood peak to another community?

If yes, the project must be redesigned or paired with storage/attenuation measures.

### 4.3 Blue-green infrastructure network

Potential elements:

- protected/restored wetlands;
- mangrove belts;
- riparian buffers;
- floodplain reconnection;
- detention/retention basins;
- floodable parks and sports fields;
- bioswales and infiltration strips;
- rain gardens;
- permeable surfaces;
- urban tree canopy;
- green roofs where structurally/economically justified;
- rainwater harvesting;
- restored creeks/esteros;
- coastal dunes/vegetation where suitable.

Each nature-based measure needs engineering performance criteria: storage volume, infiltration, design storm, maintenance, sediment/waste loading and failure mode.

### 4.4 Structural flood defenses

Use where justified:

- levees/dikes;
- floodwalls;
- pumping stations;
- gates/tidal barriers;
- diversion channels;
- revetments/bank protection;
- sea walls/shore protection;
- elevated critical roads/bridges;
- culvert/bridge hydraulic upgrades.

Every structure requires:

- design standard and climate assumption;
- residual-risk map;
- failure/consequence analysis;
- inspection cycle;
- maintenance funding;
- backup power where active equipment exists;
- operating authority and trigger rules.

### 4.5 Urban drainage digital inventory

Every city/municipality should map:

> inlet → lateral pipe → trunk drain → box culvert → creek/estero → pump/gate → river/lake/sea outfall

For every component:

- geometry/diameter/capacity;
- invert/elevation;
- material/age;
- owner;
- condition;
- obstruction/sediment level;
- last inspection/cleaning;
- downstream dependency;
- known surcharge/flood history.

This converts “bahain dito” into a diagnosable hydraulic network.

### 4.6 Drainage maintenance standard

Required minimum:

- risk-based cleaning frequency;
- CCTV/inspection for buried drains where appropriate;
- pump preventive-maintenance schedule;
- debris screens with safe bypass/failure design;
- sediment removal;
- publicly visible maintenance history for major assets;
- pre-typhoon readiness inspection.

Maintenance must be funded as a recurring service, not only through emergency clearing.

### 4.7 Solid waste as hydraulic capacity

Measure:

- waste generated;
- collection coverage;
- transfer/disposal capacity;
- illegal dumping;
- waste reaching drainage/waterways;
- recurring blockage locations;
- enforcement and cleanup response.

A drainage project repeatedly blocked by unmanaged waste is not “completed” in outcome terms.

### 4.8 Sewerage and water quality

Flood and drainage engineering must avoid turning waterways into sewage conveyance.

Integrate:

- sewer/septic strategies;
- wastewater treatment;
- illegal discharge enforcement;
- combined-stress planning during floods;
- public-health monitoring after flood events.

### 4.9 River encroachment and easements

Use lawful, rights-respecting processes.

Resolution sequence:

1. survey the actual hydraulic corridor and legal easements;
2. distinguish critical obstruction from non-critical occupation;
3. provide due process;
4. where relocation is required, apply the Resettlement + Mobility + Livelihood + Services gate;
5. restore the corridor and prevent re-encroachment;
6. maintain public access/greenway where appropriate.

### 4.10 Dams, reservoirs and irrigation coordination

Model reservoir operation as part of basin management:

- inflow forecasts;
- storage;
- irrigation demand;
- water supply;
- hydropower where applicable;
- environmental flow;
- downstream flood risk;
- drought reserve.

Use predefined decision rules and public communication appropriate to safety/security constraints.

### 4.11 Coastal and storm-surge resilience

For coastal cities/islands:

- surge maps under sea-level-rise scenarios;
- mangrove/wetland protection;
- setback/no-build zones where justified;
- raised/robust evacuation and critical facilities;
- resilient ports/ferry terminals;
- selective hard defenses;
- safe evacuation routes and vertical evacuation where appropriate;
- managed retreat for repeatedly indefensible locations.

### 4.12 Groundwater, subsidence and land elevation

Where groundwater extraction contributes to subsidence or worsens flood exposure:

- monitor land elevation;
- integrate water-allocation policy;
- reduce unsustainable extraction;
- expand surface-water/reuse options where feasible;
- adjust flood models as ground level changes.

## 5. Mobility engineering: move people, not congestion

### 5.1 National accessibility metric

Replace simple road-speed obsession with:

> How many people can reach jobs, schools, hospitals, markets and government services within 30/60/90 minutes at reasonable cost?

Disaggregate by:

- income;
- gender where relevant;
- disability/accessibility;
- age;
- urban/rural;
- normal vs hazard conditions.

### 5.2 Walking first/last mile

Minimum urban network:

- continuous sidewalks;
- safe crossings;
- accessibility ramps/tactile features;
- lighting;
- shade where feasible;
- drainage that does not turn sidewalks into channels;
- obstruction enforcement;
- safe access to schools and transit.

A rail station with an unsafe 700-meter walk is not fully connected.

### 5.3 Cycling and micromobility

Build protected, connected facilities where demand, road geometry and safety support them.

Network, not fragments:

- protected corridors;
- intersections;
- secure parking;
- station access;
- school/work connections;
- speed management.

### 5.4 Bus priority before waiting for every rail project

Candidate measures:

- median busways;
- dedicated curb/median lanes;
- queue jumps;
- signal priority;
- high-capacity stops/stations;
- off-board or faster fare collection;
- all-door boarding where operationally supported;
- frequent service;
- service contracts/performance standards where policy supports them;
- real-time passenger information.

Bus priority can deliver high person-throughput faster than major rail construction on suitable corridors.

### 5.5 PUV/jeepney integration

Goal: professionalize without treating drivers/operators as disposable.

Use:

- route hierarchy;
- feeder/trunk design;
- cooperative/fleet governance;
- service quality and frequency standards;
- safe/clean vehicles;
- interoperable information/fare systems where feasible;
- financial/skills transition support;
- designated stopping/terminal rules.

### 5.6 Urban rail

Rail investment gates:

- demonstrated passenger demand;
- network role;
- right-of-way feasibility;
- station catchment and feeder plan;
- utility relocation plan;
- flood/seismic/volcanic resilience;
- operation/maintenance funding;
- rolling-stock/depot plan;
- integration with land use;
- lifecycle cost.

Avoid standalone prestige lines without strong network integration.

### 5.7 Regional/commuter rail

Prioritize corridors where rail can:

- connect dense regional centers;
- serve commuter belts;
- shift intercity trips from road;
- carry freight where technically/economically justified;
- connect ports and logistics hubs.

### 5.8 Integrated fares and passenger information

Long-term target:

> walk/bike → feeder → bus/rail/ferry → transfer → destination

as one understandable network.

Components:

- interoperable fare/account architecture;
- coordinated maps and journey planning;
- unified service alerts;
- transfer rules;
- accessibility information;
- passenger rights/service standards.

### 5.9 Transit-oriented development

Around high-capacity transit:

- mixed housing/jobs/services;
- walkable blocks;
- lower parking dependency;
- public space;
- affordable housing;
- universal accessibility;
- safe cycling;
- adequate drainage/water/sewerage/power.

Do **not** add density where utilities and hazard capacity cannot support it.

### 5.10 Parking and road-space management

Where high-quality alternatives exist:

- price scarce curb/parking space;
- manage loading zones;
- enforce no-parking on critical access corridors;
- dedicate road space to higher person-throughput modes;
- consider congestion/road-user pricing only with equity, public-transport and legal/fiscal design.

### 5.11 Traffic operations

Use:

- adaptive/coordinated signals where justified;
- bus/emergency priority;
- incident detection;
- quick clearance;
- curb management;
- construction traffic plans;
- school-zone management;
- data-driven enforcement.

Avoid using signal optimization to merely push queues into downstream bottlenecks.

## 6. Archipelagic transport system

### 6.1 Ferries as public transport

For island and coastal communities, a reliable ferry can be more important than a large highway.

Service planning must include:

- minimum service frequency;
- safety and vessel standards;
- weather thresholds;
- passenger information;
- accessible terminals;
- bus/rail/PUV connections;
- emergency alternatives.

### 6.2 Port hierarchy

Classify ports by function:

- national gateway;
- regional passenger/freight;
- local inter-island;
- fisheries;
- emergency/logistics reserve.

Investment follows network role, not political visibility alone.

### 6.3 Airport hierarchy

Airports serve:

- long-distance passenger access;
- tourism/business;
- remote/island connectivity;
- emergency logistics.

Require:

- surface public-transport access;
- resilient power/comms/water;
- hazard closure scenarios;
- alternate-airport contingency;
- realistic demand.

### 6.4 Roll-on/Roll-off and road-ferry continuity

Map the national chain:

> road → port → vessel → port → road

A broken ferry schedule, port ramp or connecting road can disable an entire corridor.

## 7. Freight and food logistics

### 7.1 Strategic freight corridors

Identify:

- ports;
- industrial/economic zones;
- farms;
- food terminals;
- cold chain;
- warehouses;
- airports;
- rail freight where feasible.

Protect freight movement without sacrificing dense passenger corridors.

### 7.2 Urban freight

Use:

- designated truck routes;
- delivery windows;
- loading zones;
- consolidation hubs;
- off-peak operations where beneficial;
- enforcement against blocking critical lanes/sidewalks.

### 7.3 Food resilience

Connect:

> production → farm access → consolidation → cold/storage → port/road/rail → urban markets

Model flood/landslide/typhoon failure of each link.

## 8. Housing, land use and relocation

### 8.1 No new hazard debt

New settlements and subdivisions must pass:

- flood;
- landslide;
- seismic/liquefaction;
- storm surge/sea-level rise;
- water supply;
- drainage/sewerage;
- power;
- public transport;
- school/health access.

### 8.2 Housing + transport affordability

Measure household burden as:

> housing cost + transport cost + travel time risk

Cheap distant housing that destroys livelihood/access can be socially expensive.

### 8.3 Resettlement gate

Before NHA/LGU/other public relocation:

1. legal/due-process basis;
2. origin risk and necessity;
3. destination multi-hazard screen;
4. potable water and sanitation;
5. power/telecom;
6. school capacity;
7. health access;
8. jobs/livelihood;
9. frequent public transport;
10. walking accessibility;
11. household travel-cost test;
12. social/community support;
13. tenure/security;
14. post-relocation monitoring.

### 8.4 Informal settlements and waterways

Do not reduce the problem to demolition.

Combine:

- in-situ upgrading where safe;
- drainage/waste services;
- tenure solutions where lawful;
- river/easement restoration where essential;
- nearby/in-city or transit-connected resettlement;
- livelihood preservation.

## 9. Emergency accessibility network

BFP + DOH + PNP + DPWH + DOTr + LGUs + OCD should model access from every populated area to:

- fire/rescue;
- hospital/trauma care;
- evacuation center;
- police/public safety;
- major relief/logistics hub;
- port/airport where relevant.

Run two networks:

1. **normal condition**;
2. **hazard-degraded condition**.

Identify:

- single bridges;
- flood-prone underpasses;
- landslide chokepoints;
- narrow roads;
- roads unusable by fire engines/ambulances;
- ferry dependencies;
- power-dependent signals/gates;
- evacuation centers themselves inside hazards.

A community with one vulnerable access bridge has a measurable resilience deficit.

## 10. Critical-facility engineering

### 10.1 Hospitals

Require:

- flood-safe access;
- structural/hazard resilience;
- emergency power;
- potable water reserve;
- communications redundancy;
- oxygen/medicine supply continuity;
- patient transfer routes;
- drainage and infection-control planning.

### 10.2 Fire stations

Require:

- hazard-safe siting;
- response catchment coverage;
- alternate routes;
- reliable water/hydrants;
- backup power/comms;
- rescue equipment appropriate to local hazards.

### 10.3 Schools

Classify:

- ordinary school only;
- suitable evacuation facility;
- unsuitable for evacuation.

Suitability needs structural, flood, fire, accessibility, sanitation, water and capacity checks.

### 10.4 Jails/corrections

Require secure evacuation/transfer routes, backup utilities and continuity while preserving rights and security.

### 10.5 Government data centers/command centers

Avoid single physical and network failure paths; maintain tested disaster recovery and cyber resilience.

## 11. Power, water and telecom resilience

### 11.1 Critical-load register

Map critical loads:

- pumping stations;
- hospitals;
- BFP/command centers;
- rail signaling/control;
- traffic management;
- ports/airports;
- water treatment/pumps;
- telecom nodes;
- evacuation centers.

Each critical load should have:

- primary supply;
- alternate supply/backup;
- fuel/autonomy where generators exist;
- restoration priority;
- flood/seismic exposure.

### 11.2 Water resilience graph

Map:

> source → treatment → storage → pump → transmission → distribution → critical customer

Run drought, flood, contamination, power-loss and earthquake scenarios.

### 11.3 Telecom resilience

Require:

- backup power;
- diverse backhaul where feasible;
- emergency restoration priorities;
- cell/site hazard exposure;
- public warning capacity;
- satellite/alternate communications for isolated response where justified.

## 13. Loop engineering

### 13.1 Flood learning loop

> forecast → prepare → event → observe → respond → measure actual flood → compare with model → diagnose failure → update maintenance/design → revise capital plan

Store both predicted and observed flood extent/depth/duration where data quality allows.

### 13.2 Mobility learning loop

> demand → service plan → operate → passenger counts → travel time → reliability → crowding → safety/complaints → adjust service/network

Public transport is an operating service, not only an infrastructure project.

### 13.3 Asset lifecycle loop

> inspect → score condition → prioritize maintenance → execute → verify → update condition → predict next intervention

### 13.4 Budget/outcome loop

> intended outcome → appropriation → procurement → delivery → actual cost → actual outcome → evaluation → next budget

### 13.5 Disaster recovery loop

> damage → rapid assessment → temporary restoration → build-back decision → resilient reconstruction → after-action review → standards update

- asset/corridor served;
- program/master-plan relationship;
- funding source;
- appropriation references;

- milestones;

## 15. Cross-agency duplication and double-funding analysis

Classify overlap before escalation.

### Class 1 — Legitimate complementarity

Example: national highway rehabilitation plus barangay access road.

### Class 2 — Coordinated co-financing

Different funders/implementers deliver distinct components of one program.

### Class 3 — Geographic intersection

Projects overlap spatially but scopes differ.

### Class 4 — Scope duplication

Two projects appear to fund the same deliverable/asset segment.

### Class 5 — Possible double funding or incompatible claims

Same/similar asset + same scope + overlapping period + funding/payment claims inconsistent with one another.

Only Classes 4–5 normally justify stronger verification. Even then:

> overlap signal ≠ fraud finding.

## 16. Data architecture: one digital government, not one giant database

### 16.1 Authoritative-source principle

Examples:

- PSA — canonical statistics;
- NAMRIA — national geospatial base;
- PAGASA — weather/hydrometeorology;
- PHIVOLCS — seismic/volcanic/tsunami hazards;
- DBM — budget/appropriation/release systems;
- PhilGEPS — procurement;
- DEPDev — planning/public-investment/monitoring;
- agency systems — operational assets/projects;
- LGU systems — local plans, assets and permits;
- COA — audit information under constitutional authority.

The integration layer stores references/provenance and normalized views where justified; it does not declare itself the source of truth for everything.

### 16.2 Interoperability contract

Each participating system should expose, as legally appropriate:

- stable identifiers;
- update timestamp/version;
- geographic reference;
- lifecycle/status vocabulary;
- provenance/source;
- uncertainty/quality;
- access classification;
- change history.

### 16.3 Privacy and security tiers

Separate:

- open/public infrastructure data;
- internal operational data;
- personal/protected data;
- sensitive critical-infrastructure/security data;
- investigative/audit-confidential data.

A public transparency portal must never become a raw dump of sensitive assurance graphs.

## 17. Procurement and delivery engineering

### 17.1 Planning before tender

No tender until:

- scope/geometry is stable enough;
- dependencies are identified;
- ROW strategy exists;
- utility relocations are planned;
- environmental/hazard gates are passed;
- operations/maintenance owner is identified;
- lifecycle budget is credible.

### 17.2 Contractor performance

Track verified:

- schedule performance;
- cost variation;
- defects;
- materials/quality tests;
- safety;
- warranty;
- repeat repairs;
- maintenance performance where contracted.

Use this for risk-based verification and procurement policy consistent with law, not public accusation.

### 17.3 Change-order controls

Every variation should preserve:

- reason;
- initiating condition;
- quantity/cost impact;
- time impact;
- approving authority;
- evidence;
- relation to original scope.

Repeated predictable “unforeseen” conditions should trigger planning/process review.

## 18. Maintenance-first asset management

### 18.1 National maintenance backlog

Each infrastructure owner should publish/maintain internally at minimum:

- asset condition;
- criticality;
- failure consequence;
- last maintenance;
- next required maintenance;
- estimated intervention cost;
- backlog risk.

### 18.2 Risk-based prioritization

Priority score can consider:

- life safety;
- service population;
- network redundancy;
- critical-service dependency;
- hazard exposure;
- condition;
- economic importance;
- equity.

Do not use a black-box AI score as an authoritative allocation decision. The formula, inputs and tradeoffs must be explainable and governable.

## 19. Nature, climate and “do no new harm” gates

Before final approval, each major project asks:

1. Does it increase downstream flood risk?
2. Does it destroy flood storage/wetlands/mangroves without adequate justification/mitigation?
3. Does it induce settlement in a high-risk zone?
4. Does it lock the country into high operating/maintenance burden?
5. Does it worsen public-transport accessibility?
6. Does it create new displacement without a viable resettlement solution?
7. Is it robust to plausible 2050 climate conditions?
8. Is there a lower-cost nature-based, operational or demand-management alternative?
9. Is the residual risk explicitly communicated?

## 20. Metropolitan engineering

Functional metro areas need cross-LGU plans for:

- trunk public transport;
- road hierarchy;
- traffic signals/incident management;
- drainage catchments;
- rivers/waterways;
- solid-waste leakage;
- housing growth;
- freight;
- emergency access;
- air quality;
- water and power capacity.

NCR uses MMDA's statutory framework. Other metros should use existing/future lawful institutions, RDC mechanisms and inter-LGU arrangements rather than blindly copying NCR.

## 21. Rural and island engineering

Do not apply Metro Manila solutions everywhere.

Rural/island priorities may be:

- resilient provincial/local roads;
- landslide-safe mountain corridors;
- reliable ferries and small ports;
- farm/fish logistics;
- small bridges;
- water supply;
- distributed renewable/backup energy;
- digital connectivity;
- evacuation access;
- climate-resilient agriculture;
- targeted aviation.

The correct solution is the **minimum robust network** that delivers accessibility, not the maximum amount of concrete.

- agency source-system map;
- basin/metropolitan/corridor priority map;
- critical-facility accessibility baseline;
- drainage/road/bridge/rail/port/airport asset-data minimums;
- mandatory O&M-owner field for new projects;
- initial cross-agency overlap/duplication verification rules;
- policy for public vs protected data.

Do not wait for perfect nationwide data before starting. Begin with high-risk basins/metropolitan areas and synthetic/verified pilots.

### Phase 1 — 2027–2030: repair, maintain and remove bottlenecks

Prioritize:

- drainage clearing/rehabilitation;
- pump reliability/backup power;
- bridge/culvert bottlenecks;
- road/rail asset maintenance;
- bus priority and station access;
- emergency-access chokepoints;
- hospital/fire-station continuity;
- high-risk housing/evacuation deficiencies;
- solid-waste leakage into waterways.

These often produce faster benefits than only launching megaprojects.

### Phase 2 — 2031–2035: build network transformations

Scale:

- BRT/busways;
- rail expansions where justified;
- intermodal terminals;
- regional rail;
- ferry/port networks;
- major basin storage/floodplain restoration;
- strategic flood defenses;
- wastewater/sewerage;
- transit-oriented housing;
- resilient energy/water/telecom upgrades.

### Phase 3 — 2036–2040: deepen metropolitan and regional integration

- integrated fares/information;
- mature TOD;
- freight/logistics hubs;
- cross-LGU basin and transport authorities/agreements where needed;
- widespread predictive maintenance;
- climate-driven redesign of vulnerable assets.

### Phase 4 — 2041–2050: adapt to observed climate, demographic and technology shifts

- retire/replace obsolete assets;
- managed retreat where necessary;
- reconfigure networks as population/economic geography changes;
- upgrade standards using observed disaster performance;
- preserve long-term fiscal and ecological sustainability.

## 23. Prioritization framework

Each candidate intervention receives transparent scores/assessment across:

- lives/safety;
- people served;
- accessibility gain;
- flood-risk reduction;
- critical-service continuity;
- climate robustness;
- ecological impact;
- equity/poverty effect;
- lifecycle cost;
- readiness/dependencies;
- O&M capacity;
- network value;
- economic productivity;
- uncertainty/evidence quality.

No single score should hide tradeoffs. Publish the factor-level reasoning where appropriate.

## 25. Adversarial tests for the master plan

Before accepting a policy/project recommendation, ask:

1. Are we assuming duplication merely because two projects are nearby?
2. Are we treating missing data as proof something did not happen?
3. Are we optimizing one LGU by shifting flood/traffic to another?
4. Are we prioritizing private-car speed over person-throughput/access?
5. Are we relocating people away from flood risk but into transport poverty?
6. Are we building new capacity while existing assets deteriorate?
7. Are we depending on one power/telecom/bridge/port node?
8. Are climate assumptions obsolete before the asset reaches mid-life?
9. Are we creating a new database that duplicates an authoritative system?
10. Are we exposing personal/security-sensitive data in the name of transparency?
11. Are we using AI/risk scoring as a legal or budget authority rather than decision support?
12. Are we measuring completion rather than the intended outcome?
13. Are we naming an agency as owner when it lacks the legal mandate?
14. Are we excluding BARMM/LGUs or overriding autonomy?
15. Are we forgetting operations and maintenance after construction?

## 26. Specific national program packages

### Package A — Emergency Access 60

Goal: materially reduce communities that cannot reach fire/rescue/hospital services within defined target times.

Actions:

- model catchments;
- fix critical bridge/road/ferry chokepoints;
- add BFP/EMS stations where coverage gaps justify;
- protect emergency corridors from chronic obstruction;
- harden routes against recurrent flood/landslide.

### Package B — Drainage Truth

Goal: every major urban drainage system becomes mapped, inspectable and maintainable.

Actions:

- geospatial drainage inventory;
- ownership/capacity/condition;
- blockage/cleaning history;
- pump telemetry where useful;
- flood complaint linkage;
- public maintenance performance for major assets.

### Package C — Basin Resilience

Goal: coordinate upstream land, rivers, irrigation, floodplain and coastal interventions.

Actions:

- priority-basin models;
- storage/restoration;
- structural protection where justified;
- dam/irrigation operating coordination;
- downstream-impact gates.

### Package D — Public Transport First

Goal: rapidly increase person-throughput and accessibility before defaulting to road widening.

Actions:

- bus priority;
- route reform;
- feeder integration;
- station access;
- fare/information interoperability;
- TOD;
- rail where demand warrants.

### Package E — Archipelago Link

Goal: treat ferries, ports, airports and connecting roads/public transport as one accessibility chain.

Actions:

- ferry service standards;
- resilient port hierarchy;
- intermodal schedules;
- remote-area emergency alternatives;
- food/freight continuity.

### Package F — Safe Housing Access

Goal: no public housing/relocation project creates a new hazard or mobility trap.

Actions:

- mandatory hazard/access gate;
- housing+transport affordability;
- services before occupancy;
- post-relocation outcome monitoring.

### Package G — Maintain What We Own

Goal: shift budget and management attention toward asset condition and preventive maintenance.

Actions:

- asset register;
- maintenance backlog;
- criticality score;
- lifecycle budget;
- condition/outage outcome reporting.

### Package H — Trace the Public Peso to the Public Outcome

Goal: enable lawful, evidence-based traceability:

> plan → appropriation → procurement → contract → asset → inspection → payment → maintenance → outcome

Use this for transparency, planning and risk-based verification while maintaining the firewall between a signal and a legal/audit finding.

## 27. Relationship to eGovTrace

The engineering plan does not mean eGovTrace becomes the national infrastructure operating system.

eGovTrace's appropriate role is narrower and safer:

- represent expected control paths;
- ingest or reference observed government events from authoritative/synthetic sources;
- reconcile expected vs observed;
- preserve provenance and uncertainty;
- detect explainable control/anomaly signals;
- show missing information;
- support human verification;
- maintain a graph of relationships without implying wrongdoing;
- preserve downstream accountability and legal authority boundaries.

The national master-plan research expands the future **State Control Universe** and gives eGovTrace candidate domains to model later, but implementation must remain staged.

## 28. Research-to-engineering promotion sequence

For any proposed eGovTrace feature derived from this plan:

1. choose one bounded government process/problem;
2. identify authoritative law/mandate and source systems;
3. define expected lifecycle/control path;
4. create synthetic fixtures before production integration;
5. define event/provenance semantics;
6. define reconciliation semantics, including NOT_OBSERVED vs ABSENT and UNAVAILABLE;
7. define deterministic, non-accusatory detection rules;
8. define human verification;
9. only then consider persistence, connectors, API/UI and public projection;
10. run adversarial review before merge.

This prevents the master plan from turning into an unbounded software scope.

## 30. Final engineering success test

By 2050, success is **not**:

- number of flood-control projects;
- kilometers of roads;
- number of rail lines;
- peso value obligated;
- number of ribbon cuttings.

Success is whether:

- fewer people and critical facilities suffer destructive flooding;
- flood duration and depth fall where intervention is justified;
- watersheds, wetlands and mangroves are healthier;
- public transport is faster, safer, more reliable and more accessible;
- people spend less time and money reaching jobs/services;
- housing policy stops creating remote mobility traps;
- emergency services remain reachable during hazards;
- infrastructure condition improves rather than decays;
- public money can be traced through delivery to the physical asset and intended outcome;
- government can learn from each disaster, audit, project and operating year;
- citizens can distinguish **a risk signal that needs verification** from **a proven finding**.

That is the engineering standard this handoff proposes.
